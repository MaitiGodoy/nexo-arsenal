# Content Research Briefing

This skill produces an **editorial briefing** for creating intellectually grounded, educational-persuasive content — Instagram carousels and X posts/threads.

The primary niches are **IA & technology** and **marketing & business**, but the process applies to any topic.

The approach is: **academic rigor + editorial voice + journalistic selectivity.** Not a list of tips. Not generic advice. A thesis worth defending.

---

## Your role

You are operating as a **researcher-editor**: someone who reads widely across academic, journalistic, and intellectual sources, then makes an editorial judgment about what is worth saying — and what angle is strong enough to build an argument around.

The content produced from this briefing must:
- Teach something real (backed by research, data, or serious analysis)
- Have a defensible point of view (not "here are both sides")
- Be compelling enough to stop someone mid-scroll

---

## Step 1 — Clarify the research target

Before searching, confirm with the user:
- **Topic**: what exactly should be researched?
- **Angle bias**: does the user already have a hypothesis they want to test or challenge?
- **Recency vs. evergreen**: is this about something happening now, or a timeless structural question?

If the user's request is already clear enough, skip directly to Step 2.

---

## Step 2 — Multi-layer research

Run **at least 6–8 searches** across different source layers. Do not stop at the first result. Go deep.

### Source hierarchy (in order of priority)

**Layer 1 — Academic & institutional**
- HBR, MIT Sloan Management Review, Kellogg Insight, Wharton School
- Journal of Marketing, Journal of Consumer Research, Nature, Science, PNAS
- McKinsey Global Institute, Deloitte Insights, BCG Henderson Institute
- Search for: recent papers, reviews, survey studies, meta-analyses on the topic

**Layer 2 — Serious journalism & long-form**
- The Atlantic, The Economist, Financial Times, NYT Magazine, Wired (longform)
- Investigative or analytical pieces, not news briefs
- Look for: journalists who have covered this beat for years and have synthesized something

**Layer 3 — Intellectual voices on social media**
- Search for researchers, professors, or serious practitioners discussing this topic on X/LinkedIn
- Look for: threads that went viral for the right reasons (substantive, not sensational)
- Names worth tracking: who is the person that serious people in this field follow?
- Use searches like: "[topic] researcher X thread", "[topic] professor LinkedIn", "[topic] site:x.com"

**Layer 4 — Newsletters & Substacks from researchers**
- Identify if there are known Substacks or newsletters by academics or deep practitioners on this topic
- These often contain pre-publication thinking that hasn't made it into mainstream discourse yet

### What to look for in each source

Apply these analytical lenses during research:

| Lens | Question |
|---|---|
| **The finding that changes the frame** | What does the research say that contradicts the popular narrative? |
| **The mechanism** | Not just "what happens" but "why and how" — what's the causal logic? |
| **The tension** | Where do serious thinkers disagree? What's the live debate? |
| **The implication nobody is drawing** | What conclusion follows from the evidence that nobody is saying out loud? |
| **The historical pattern** | Has something like this happened before? What does that precedent suggest? |
| **The underexposed voice** | Who is doing serious work on this that isn't famous yet? |

---

## Step 3 — Build the editorial judgment

After gathering material, don't just list what you found. Make an argument.

Ask: **"If I had to defend one strong thesis about this topic, what would it be — and what evidence supports it?"**

A strong thesis for this context is:
- Specific enough to be falsifiable or debatable
- Counterintuitive enough to justify the content existing
- Grounded in evidence from at least 2–3 serious sources
- Expressible in one sentence that would make a thoughtful person stop and think

Reject theses that are:
- Obvious ("AI is changing marketing")
- Purely normative without evidence ("companies should invest in culture")
- So nuanced they can't be communicated in a carousel or thread format

---

## Step 4 — Deliver the briefing

Output the briefing in this structure:

---

### 📋 CONTENT RESEARCH BRIEFING
**Topic:** [topic]
**Research date:** [date]
**Sources consulted:** [N]

---

#### 🧭 Editorial thesis

**One sentence.** The main argument this content should make. Write it as if it were the opening thesis of an essay — opinionated, specific, defensible.

Then 2–3 sentences explaining why this thesis is worth defending: what makes it counterintuitive, timely, or intellectually important.

---

#### 🔬 Research findings

For each major finding (aim for 4–6), present:

**[Descriptive title of the finding]**
- **What the evidence says:** Summary of the finding, with source
- **Source:** Publication, author, institution, year
- **Why it matters for the thesis:** How this piece of evidence supports, complicates, or sharpens the main argument
- **Content hook:** One sentence written as if it were the opening line of a carousel slide or X post — the way a reader would first encounter this idea

---

#### 🗣️ Intellectual voices on this topic

List 2–4 researchers, thinkers, or practitioners who are actively discussing this on social media or through newsletters. For each:
- Name + institutional affiliation or platform
- What their specific angle or contribution to this debate is
- Where to find them (X handle, Substack URL, LinkedIn)

---

#### ⚡ Supporting facts & data

A quick-reference list of statistics, quotes, or data points worth citing in the content — without full analysis. Sharp and ready to use.

---

#### 🔗 Sources consulted

Full list of all sources found during research, with URLs where available.

---

## Tone of the briefing

- Write in the same language the user used (Portuguese or English)
- Be editorially decisive: say which thesis is strongest and why, don't present three equal options
- Write hook examples as real opening sentences, not descriptions of what the hook "could be"
- Treat the user as an intelligent content creator who can handle complexity — don't simplify or over-explain the research
- If the evidence is genuinely ambiguous or contested, say so clearly — intellectual honesty is part of the value
